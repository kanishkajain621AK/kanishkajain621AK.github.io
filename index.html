The problem

A devotional-goods catalogue is unusually hard to merchandise. A customer doesn't arrive looking for "a bracelet" — they arrive looking for something appropriate to their rashi, their deity, or a ritual they've been told to perform. That cuts across a normal collection structure, and the themes flexible enough to express it came loaded with third-party JavaScript.

The constraints

  • Mobile-first. Most traffic arrives on mid-range Android over mobile data.
  • Non-technical merchandising. The catalogue team needed to re-merchandise without a developer.
  • A tight performance budget. Mobile Lighthouse performance started at 60.
  • 800 SKUs with overlapping attributes — one product belongs in several taxonomies at once.

What I built

I wrote the theme from scratch in Liquid rather than buying one — no page builder, no purchased base. Every section is one I own, which meant no inherited JavaScript to work around and no vendor upgrade path to break.

  • Shop-by-rashi and shop-by-deity merchandising across 40+ collections, so the catalogue can be browsed the way customers actually think about it rather than by product type alone.
  • A custom cart with qualified upsells — suggestions are driven by what's already in the cart rather than a fixed "you may also like" list.
  • Build Your Own Combo — a bundle builder with tiered discounting that reprices as items are added, plus three-tier ritual add-ons that attach at cart and order level.
  • Vedic recommendation calculators that turn an advice-seeking visit into a guided purchase by pointing the customer at a specific product.
  • GoKwik one-click checkout with COD verification.
  • A page-transition and motion layer and a conversion-focused product page template, both built natively rather than pulled from a library.

Performance: 60 to 90+

The largest single contributor was removing third-party apps and rebuilding what they did natively — every app removed took its script with it. Owning the theme end to end is what made the rest of the tuning possible: with no inherited code on the page, everything left is there because I put it there.

Result

A storefront doing ~US$600K/month with mobile performance at 90+, where the catalogue team re-merchandises 40+ collections without engineering involvement.

Why build it

The paid reviews app cost a subscription, injected its own script into every page, and locked the display rules and widget design to its own template. Replacing it meant owning the review data, the structured output search engines read, and the ordering rules that decide what a new visitor sees first.

What it has to handle

Ten thousand reviews across nine hundred products is past the point where anything can be done by hand. The whole design assumption is that a non-technical team runs this daily: moderation, imports, display rules and the widget's appearance all have to be theirs to change, without a developer and without a deploy.

What I built

  • A moderation queue with real actions. Approve, hide, verify, pin to top, edit or delete per review, with search across text, author and email, and filters by product, status, star rating and media.
  • Bulk import that doesn't duplicate. The Excel template ships with a product_handle dropdown of every live product, regenerated fresh from Shopify on every download, so it always reflects newly added products and drops removed ones. Re-uploading the same rows updates them instead of creating copies — which is what makes a spreadsheet safe to hand to a non-engineer.
  • Photo hosting that stays inside Shopify. Customer images upload through the dashboard and are stored on the store's own CDN, returning URLs to paste back into the import sheet — no external media host to keep alive.
  • Merchant-controlled display order. Newest first, photos first, highest rating or most helpful, with pinned reviews always ahead of the rule. That ordering decision is a conversion lever, so it belongs to the merchandising team rather than to me.
  • A widget designer with live preview. Colours, sizes and card styling apply store-wide on save, previewed in place before committing.
  • Backdating and verified badges on manual entry, plus an aggregate recompute that rebuilds every product's star totals and averages after a bulk change or an edit made directly in the Shopify admin.

Why build it

The in-house reviews platform above solves one merchant's problem. This is the same problem solved for every merchant at once — a subscription SaaS competing directly with Judge.me, Loox and Okendo. Going from single-tenant to public distribution changes almost everything: authentication, tenancy isolation, billing, data protection and the app-review process all become the product rather than plumbing around it.

What it actually has to do

Know when an order was delivered rather than paid. Email the buyer at the right moment and stop if they've already reviewed. Take photos and video from a phone without making them wait. Let the merchant moderate first. Render on any theme without hurting Lighthouse. Push the aggregate into metafields so Google and the Shop app pick it up. And never misrepresent a review's provenance — which is now illegal, not merely tacky.

Order to published review

This pipeline is the product. Every step is asynchronous, retried and independently observable, and the scheduling of work is deliberately separated from the doing of it.

What makes it hold up

  • Failure is the retry. A failed send leaves nextSendAt in the past, so the next sweep picks it up. No queue infrastructure, no dead-letter handling, and no cron that can double-send.
  • Media never blocks the response. Validation is synchronous — MIME allowlist plus magic-byte sniffing — but the upload to Shopify Files runs after the response has flushed. The buyer never waits on a CDN round trip.
  • Media lives in the merchant's own Shopify Files, not our storage. No storage cost to carry, served from Shopify's CDN, and the merchant keeps their assets if they uninstall.
  • Reminders self-cancel. A submitted review, an unsubscribe or a bounce cancels everything outstanding for that address.
  • Tenancy is checked at the query, not the route. Every authenticated query carries storeId from the verified session token and ownership is asserted before any cross-entity write, so a merchant can't reach another's data by guessing an ID.

Compliance enforced by the schema, not by policy

The FTC's Rule on Consumer Reviews (16 CFR 465) carries civil penalties north of US$50,000 per violation for conditioning an incentive on what a review says. The EU Omnibus Directive and the UK DMCC Act say the same thing.

So the incentive model has no minimum-rating field, and the grant function cannot receive a rating — the caller has no way to pass one. There is nowhere in the type system to express the illegal idea. A minRating column with a comment saying "don't set this above 1" eventually gets set; a column that doesn't exist can't. Rewarding a photo is lawful, because media is a content type rather than an opinion — a one-star review with a photo earns exactly what a five-star one with a photo earns.

The same reasoning governs provenance. verificationStatus can only ever be verified_buyer on the tokenised post-purchase path, because that is the only path with an order to point at. CSV imports, manual entry and marketplace imports are structurally incapable of claiming verification.

Authentication: the part that's genuinely hard

Embedded Shopify apps run in an iframe inside another origin's admin, where cookies are unreliable and Safari's ITP blocks them outright. The app authenticates on App Bridge session tokens — a 60-second JWT verified for issuer, destination, audience and expiry, then exchanged server-side for an offline access token. That exchange is the install: no OAuth callback, no redirect, no state nonce to get wrong. Access tokens live an hour and refresh tokens ninety days; both are encrypted at rest with AES-256-GCM. A present-but-invalid session token is a hard failure rather than a silent downgrade to cookie auth, so an attacker can't strip a header to select the weaker mechanism.

The bug worth the war story: Next's <Script strategy="beforeInteractive"> does not render into <head> in the App Router — it emits a preload plus a body-queued shim. App Bridge therefore never initialised, window.shopify stayed undefined, and every request silently fell back to cookies. The page looked completely fine. I proved the fix by writing per-store telemetry that recorded which mechanism had actually authenticated each request.

The rest of the surface

  • Collection — post-purchase requests with a 0–60 day merchant-configurable delay and up to two reminders, a token-based buyer form that needs no account, photo and video reviews, a storefront submission widget with honeypot and per-email rate limiting, and product-page Q&A.
  • Import — CSV with per-row error reporting, AliExpress listing scrape with deduplication and star normalisation, and Etsy via OAuth 2.0 PKCE with listings matched to Shopify products by title. Every imported review is source-labelled and never marked verified.
  • Display — nine widget layouts shipped as a theme app extension: 8 KB gzipped, no framework, lazy-loaded via IntersectionObserver 400px before it enters the viewport, with explicit media dimensions so it contributes no CLS. Plus JSON-LD for Google rich results and the reviews.rating metafield the Shop app reads.
  • Operations — four subscription tiers gated server-side at every write path, bounce and complaint handling through signed provider webhooks with a suppression list, automated retention (PII redacted at 30 days post-expiry, rows purged at 730), and self-healing webhook registration.

Decisions I'd defend in a review

  • The storefront widget is deliberately not React. It runs on product pages, which carry most of the weight in the Lighthouse score a merchant is judged on. Its source is heavily commented — the FTC rules, the CLS reasoning and the escaping rules all need to survive the next person to touch it — so a custom build step strips comments rather than minifying. Renaming identifiers would mean a stack trace from a merchant's storefront points at a.b(c) and is worth nothing.
  • Schema migration lives inside the deploy. prisma db push runs in CI before the container swaps, so the database is never behind the code that expects it. This replaced a recurring failure where a deploy succeeded and then threw on the first query.
  • Provider-agnostic email. One interface, three implementations, selected by environment variable. Not premature abstraction — AWS denied production SES access for a new account on an azurewebsites.net domain, and the switch was a config change plus a webhook route rather than a rewrite.
  • Build errors are not ignorable. typescript.ignoreBuildErrors is off deliberately: it had previously masked five calls to a function that did not exist, each throwing at runtime while the build went green.

What the app review actually gates on

Shopify's review process and any enterprise merchant's procurement ask the same questions, so this was built rather than promised: Protected Customer Data Level 2 approval with all sixteen data-protection questions answered against real implementation; a published DPA under GDPR Article 28 with SCCs and UK/Swiss addenda, a sub-processor list, privacy policy and terms; the three mandatory GDPR webhooks — customers/data_request, customers/redact, shop/redact — implemented rather than stubbed; and a documented retention schedule, DLP posture, incident-response runbook and environment separation. Every third-party credential is encrypted at rest.

Why build it

Off-the-shelf loyalty apps couldn't express the programme the brand actually wanted: prepaid-only cashback, a hard redemption cap, short FIFO expiry, WhatsApp nudges, and redemption happening inside a third-party one-click checkout rather than through a coupon field. Divine Points is a small, purpose-built service that owns the ledger and plugs into Shopify and GoKwik at exactly the right seams.

The programme

1 Mudra = ₹1. Earn 25% of order value, credited when the order is paid. Redeem up to 25% of an eligible cart above ₹999. Coins expire 45 days after earning, drawn down oldest-first. Cancel or refund reverses earned coins and restores redeemed ones. Every one of those values is admin-configurable at runtime — including per-slab and new-versus-repeat earn rates and per-product overrides — with no redeploy.

The redemption path

The hard part isn't the arithmetic, it's where redemption happens. Coins are spent inside GoKwik's one-click checkout, which means a third party is quoting the customer a balance my service owns.

What makes it correct

  • Every surface is separately authenticated. Four of them: the storefront API behind Shopify's signed App Proxy, a shared-secret wallet API for GoKwik, a shared-secret events API, and HMAC-verified Shopify webhooks — plus an admin portal on its own key. Secret comparisons are timing-safe.
  • Idempotency everywhere. A unique order_id on credits, checkout_id on redemptions, per-event keys on bonuses. Webhook retries can't double-credit or double-deduct.
  • Precise restores. Each earn event is a lot with its own remaining balance and expiry; a redemption records the exact lots it consumed, so a refund restores the right coins to the right lots rather than approximating.
  • Self-healing. A pending redemption auto-restores after a TTL if the checkout is abandoned. The cart-drawer path issues a one-time discount code with the same server-side cap and the same auto-restore.
  • One identity. Phone numbers are normalised to E.164 with a one-time backfill migration, so web, app and checkout resolve to a single wallet.
  • Fraud caps. Issuance caps and registration/device checks flag or block abnormal credit activity, with an append-only transaction log behind everything.

What the merchant actually operates

The service ships with its own admin console rather than a settings page. Marketing configures earn and burn rules, per-product and per-collection cashback overrides, the referral programme and the customer-facing widget's copy and colours. Operations works from transaction logs, exclusions, caps and flags, and a manual adjustment trail. Finance pulls issuance, redemption, expiry and outstanding-liability reports as CSV or Excel. Migration and balance-import tools existed so the switch off the paid SaaS could carry every existing customer balance across in one pass.

What it does

Two tiers, two pipelines, one core. The free 2-page Kundali is the lead magnet — real chart data computed from ephemeris, not templated text. The paid tier is a 120+ page personalised report written by a roster of 30+ AI astrologers, each tuned to a distinct service, generated and delivered automatically about thirty minutes after checkout. Both tiers share the chart engine, the PDF renderer and the delivery layer.

Why two services

The chart and PDF engine is Python — Swiss Ephemeris and ReportLab have no real equivalent elsewhere. The commerce and messaging automation is Node, which was already handling conversational flows. They integrate over HTTP behind a shared admin token rather than being merged: different runtimes, different scaling characteristics, independent deploys.

Pipeline 1 — free Kundali, the lead magnet

A visitor submits name, gender, birth date, time and place, and a WhatsApp number. Within minutes they get a 2-page chart PDF: a North-Indian Lagna chart with all twelve bhavas, Moon sign, Nakshatra, Ascendant, ruling planet, Nakshatra lord and current Mahadasha on page one; narrative highlights, dasha meaning, remedies keyed to the dasha lord, three product recommendations and a CTA on page two.

Two decisions in that path matter more than they look. The HTTP request never touches the database — it appends a line to a JSONL inbox and returns, so a burst of form submissions can't contend on SQLite. And field mapping is alias-tolerant: the upstream form is edited by non-engineers and field names drift, so keys are normalised before matching rather than assumed.

Pipeline 2 — the paid report

The paid report is the detailed product — 120+ pages, drawing on the astrologer roster for the service the customer bought. Delivery runs as a two-phase sweep on a 30-second tick, driven entirely by order tags rather than a queue table:

  • Generate at 28 minutes — pull birth details from line-item properties, call the chart API per report type, write the PDF URLs into the order note, tag report-ready.
  • Deliver at 30 minutes — parse those URLs, send each as a WhatsApp document template, tag report-sent or report-wa-failed.
  • Recover on demand — a report-resend tag re-sends from the already-stored URLs without regenerating anything. The trigger tag is removed before sending, so overlapping ticks can never double-send.

Tags rather than a queue because the storefront is already the source of truth for orders: tags are transactional, visible to non-engineers in the admin, and survive any redeploy of the worker. Missing birth details short-circuit to a pending state and trigger an automated WhatsApp request to the customer, rather than failing quietly.

The failure worth reading about

The messaging gateway returned HTTP 200 for template sends that WhatsApp later rejected asynchronously. The code treated 200 as success and marked records sent; the order automation then tagged orders report-sent. Every template send failed for over a month while every internal signal — database rows, order tags, dashboards — reported success.

It surfaced only on reading the provider's own message logs, where the message bodies still contained raw {{1}} placeholders: the gateway was accepting the request and silently discarding the template parameters. What I fixed, in order of importance:

  • An acknowledgement is not a delivery. 200 now means submitted and nothing more.
  • A delivery-status webhook resolves submitted into delivered or failed using the provider's own callbacks.
  • A reconciliation sweep requeues anything still unconfirmed after a grace window, so nothing sits in limbo indefinitely.
  • Provider message IDs captured on every send, so a callback can be matched back to a customer.
  • Failure counters and a degraded flag on a health endpoint — a stalled pipeline is now one HTTP request away from visible, instead of invisible for a month.

The rewrite also made the transport swappable: the BSP gateway and Meta's Cloud API are both supported and selected at runtime by which credentials are present, with automatic fallback. A vendor problem is now a configuration change rather than a code change.

Three more production lessons

  • SQLite write contention on network-backed storage. WAL mode is ineffective on the PaaS host's networked filesystem, so concurrent writes produced database is locked under real traffic. Fixed in three layers: the JSONL inbox keeps the request path off SQLite entirely, leads moved to their own database with exactly one writer, and the remaining paths retry on lock.
  • CPU-bound rendering froze the event loop. PDF generation holds the GIL, so health checks and every endpoint timed out during a render. Rendering moved to a subprocess — subprocess.run releases the GIL while waiting — with an in-process fallback so a rendering environment problem degrades rather than breaks.
  • Background workers on a PaaS host. A fork-based daemon silently never ran under the platform's process model. A plain daemon thread runs reliably, publishes a heartbeat, and the health endpoint reports its age — a dead worker is now detectable rather than merely quiet.

What I'd do differently

I chased two plausible root causes before reading the provider's raw message log, which contained the answer in plain sight. Cheap observation beats expensive inference — look at the actual bytes on the wire first.
