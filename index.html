The problem

A devotional-goods catalogue is unusually hard to merchandise. A customer doesn't arrive looking for "a bracelet" — they arrive looking for something appropriate to their rashi, their deity, a specific dosha, or a ritual they've been told to perform. Off-the-shelf collection logic can't express that, and the page-builder themes that could be styled into it were carrying so much third-party JavaScript that mobile performance sat at 60.

The constraints

  • Mobile-first, low-bandwidth. Most traffic arrives on mid-range Android over patchy mobile data.
  • Non-technical merchandising. The catalogue team had to re-merchandise without a developer.
  • No performance budget to spare. Every app that injects a script competes with revenue.
  • 800 SKUs with deep attribute overlap — one product legitimately belongs in six taxonomies.

What I built

I wrote the theme from scratch in Liquid rather than buying one — no page builder, no purchased base. That decision is the reason the rest was possible: every section is one I own, so there was no inherited JavaScript to fight and no vendor upgrade path to break.

  • Metaobject-driven taxonomy. Rashi, deity, material and ritual are modelled as metaobjects rather than hard-coded collections, so a product can carry all of them at once and the merchandising team can create a new axis without touching code.
  • Custom cart on the Ajax Cart API with qualified upsells — offers are evaluated against current cart contents and customer state, so the drawer suggests what's actually complementary rather than a static "you may also like" list.
  • Build Your Own Combo — a bundle builder with tiered discounting that reprices in real time as items are added, plus three-tier ritual add-ons that attach at cart and order level.
  • Vedic recommendation calculators that take birth details and return a specific product recommendation, turning an advice-seeking visit into a guided purchase.
  • GoKwik one-click checkout with COD verification, which is where the RTO work (below) hooks in.
  • A page-transition and motion layer built natively rather than pulled from a library.

How performance went from 60 to 90+

The gain wasn't one trick, it was refusing to accept the usual costs:

  • Removing third-party apps and rebuilding what they did natively (see the next case study) — the single largest contributor, because each app removed took its render-blocking script with it.
  • Serving responsive images through Shopify's image CDN at explicit sizes, so the LCP element is never a full-resolution asset scaled down in the browser.
  • Deferring everything not required for first paint, and inlining only the critical path.
  • Reserving layout space for every async element so the motion layer costs nothing in CLS.

Result

A storefront doing ~US$600K/month with mobile performance at 90+, where the catalogue team re-merchandises 40+ collections without engineering involvement, and where every remaining script on the page is one I chose to put there.

The problem

Reviews and loyalty were running on subscription apps costing over US$1,000 a month combined. The cost was the visible problem. The real problem was that each app injected its own render-blocking JavaScript, neither could express the rules the business actually wanted, and the loyalty logic in particular had to be requested from support every time it needed a change.

Why build rather than switch

  • The performance work only holds if nothing re-adds vendor scripts later.
  • The loyalty rules are specific to the business — tier thresholds tied to ritual purchases, points on services as well as products. No off-the-shelf app models that.
  • Owning the review data means owning the structured-data output that search engines read.
  • At US$12k/year, the build paid for itself immediately and keeps paying.

What I built

  • Reviews platform — capture with photo and video, a moderation queue, verified-purchase matching against orders, and review-rich structured data emitted server-side so it's indexable rather than injected after paint.
  • Loyalty and rewards programme — points accrual, tiers, redemption rules and expiry, all evaluated against order webhooks, with balances surfaced on the storefront through a theme app extension rather than a third-party embed.
  • One admin, built for non-engineers. Every rule, tier, threshold and moderation action is exposed in an interface the catalogue and support teams use directly. This was the requirement that shaped the whole build — a system only an engineer can change is a system that doesn't get changed.

The problem

Personalised astrology reports are genuinely valuable to the customer and genuinely unscalable by hand — a human astrologer produces one detailed reading in days, and the business needed to sell them at ₹299 with a 24-hour promise. A single general-purpose prompt can't do it either: the output drifts, contradicts itself across sections, and doesn't hold to the specific tradition each service is sold under.

The constraints

  • Deterministic chart maths. Planetary positions are computation, not generation — the LLM must never be asked to calculate them.
  • Internal consistency across 120+ pages. Section 40 can't contradict section 3.
  • Per-service voice and doctrine. A career reading and a kundli-matching reading follow different rules.
  • Hard delivery SLA with no human in the loop.

The architecture

The core design decision is the separation between computation and generation. The chart is computed deterministically from birth date, time and place; the AI layer only ever interprets a chart it is handed. That single boundary is what makes the output trustworthy — the facts are never hallucinated because the facts are never generated.

  • 30+ agent definitions, each pinned to a service and a tradition, with its own persona, doctrinal constraints and output contract. They're configuration, not code — I can add or retune an astrologer from the dashboard without a deploy.
  • A routing layer that maps the ordered product to the right agent set.
  • Sectioned generation with a shared chart context, so every section is written against the same computed facts and prior conclusions rather than independently.
  • Assembly and typesetting into a 120+ page PDF, then automated delivery.
  • A queue-backed pipeline — orders land, generation runs asynchronously, and delivery fires on completion, well inside the 24-hour promise.

The problem

Indian COD e-commerce lives and dies on return-to-origin. Every shipment that comes back costs forward freight, return freight and handling, and the margin on the ones that land has to cover all of it. RTO isn't evenly distributed either: it clusters by pincode and by carrier, and the same carrier that's excellent in one district is unreliable two districts over — so a single national carrier choice is wrong almost everywhere.

The insight

Most stores pick a courier per order by cost, or by a fixed priority list. But the useful question isn't "who is cheapest" — it's "who actually delivers successfully to this specific pincode". That's an empirical question, and every past shipment is a data point answering it. Nobody was using that data, because it sits scattered across carrier dashboards.

What I built

  • Geolocation capture on the storefront — the browser resolves the customer's coordinates, which resolve to a pincode, before the carrier decision is made.
  • A per-pincode performance table built from historical shipment outcomes: delivery success rate, RTO rate and turnaround time, per carrier, per area.
  • A scoring model that ranks every serviceable carrier for the destination, weighted toward RTO avoidance rather than raw cost.
  • Automatic assignment at order creation, so the decision happens without anyone in operations touching it.
  • A feedback loop — every completed shipment updates the scores, so the model tracks carriers whose performance in an area changes over time.

Result

Return-to-origin fell substantially after rollout, and keeps improving as the score table grows. On a COD business this is among the highest-leverage systems in the stack, because every avoided return is forward freight, return freight and handling that never gets spent — margin recovered without selling anything more.
